Alignment Design Guideline

Based on DB-Ril 800.0110 and TSI Infrastructure
compiled by Jonathan Wolf

Introduction

This guideline presents the most important fundamentals for planning new infrastructure in the field of track alignment design. It deliberately limits itself to the basic alignment techniques in order to keep the application as simple as possible. In particularly tight spatial conditions or where demanding requirements apply, the infrastructure should still be laid out by experienced alignment designers. The aim is to enable implementation-ready planning at an early project stage with the help of this guideline and the Open Layout Tool. In this way, the planning process can be made both faster and higher in quality.

Straight

The straight represents the simplest element in track alignment design. A straight on its own has no restrictions. However, since curves usually follow, a minimum element length must be observed. This minimum element length must also be observed for circular curves and transition curves.

Circular curve

In order for an alignment to fit the geographic conditions, circular curves are needed in addition to straights. These elements can only be traversed at a certain maximum speed, since lateral acceleration increases with increasing speed. Exceeding the permissible lateral acceleration must be avoided.

Equilibrium cant

For good running dynamics, there should be no lateral acceleration even on the curve. This condition is described by the equilibrium cant u0. It is achieved by raising the outer rail of the curve, establishing a balance of forces:

u0=11.8⋅v2r
r= radius
v= speed

However, since slower trains also run, or trains may need to stop on the curve, a cant should be chosen that adequately covers both conditions. This is referred to as the regular cant ureg:

ureg=6.5⋅v2r
r= radius
v= speed

Cant deficiency

Accordingly, there is always a difference between the equilibrium cant and the applied cant, since not all trains traverse the curve at the same speed. This difference is referred to as the cant deficiency uf and represents a measure of the lateral acceleration.

This value must not exceed the permissible limits. This results in the constraint that a curve has a minimum radius for a desired speed:

r=11.8⋅v2u+uf
v= speed
u= cant
uf= cant deficiency

Transition curves

A direct transition from a straight into a circular curve creates a curvature jump. The greater the change in curvature, the stronger the abrupt change in lateral acceleration. Such a jump is unfavourable for running dynamics and must therefore be kept as small as possible.

The preferred approach is to use transition curves, which completely avoid the curvature jump. In this case the lateral acceleration is increased continuously.

Comparison radius for curvature jumps

If there is not enough space for a transition curve, the so-called comparison radius must be maintained. This ensures that the jump in lateral acceleration is not too large. An acceptable lateral acceleration is defined at a uf≈106.5 mm.

Curvature profiles

Curvature jumps should be avoided and replaced by a transition curve instead. In a transition curve the curvature increases continuously — and thus so does the lateral acceleration. Transition curves can be defined using two formulas.

Clothoid

In a clothoid, the curvature increases linearly. It can therefore be calculated in plan very easily and should be used as the standard.

κ(l)=κe⋅lL

Bloss curve

In a Bloss curve, the curvature increases according to a cubic function. As a result, no kink occurs at the beginning and end of the element.

κ(l)=κe⋅(3(lL)2−2(lL)3)

Cant transition ramps

Just as the curvature in a transition curve increases according to the defined formula, the cant increases along the cant transition ramp. In order for the cant to match the curvature, the cant transition ramp and the transition curve should have the same length and formula. In most cases, the cant transition ramp is the governing element for determining the transition curve.

Transition curves must not exceed a defined gradient. This gradient can be used to calculate a minimum length. In addition, the minimum length from the Straight section must also be observed.

Switches

From an alignment design perspective, switches (turnouts) are simply two coupled elements with a defined length. This guideline presents the most common and simplest switch types.

Geometric factors

Switches should always be chosen in the simplest possible form.

Important: From a geometric point of view, switches are only defined up to the end of the switch. However, the position of the last through sleeper (ldS) should always be taken into account as well.

Curved switches

In curved switches, the switch is not located on a straight but on a circular curve with radius R. The resulting radius rZ of the diverging track is calculated differently depending on the position of the diverging track:

Outside-curve switch

rZ=r⋅RR−r
r= switch radius
R= curve radius

Inside-curve switch

rZ=r⋅RR+r
r= switch radius
R= curve radius
